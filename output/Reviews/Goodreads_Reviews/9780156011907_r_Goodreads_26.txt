By the title and length, the latter over twice any other by the author I've read, it seems possible that Higgins may have wrote this being both aware of his fatal illness and intending to leave a sort of final literary testament on his particular style of crime fiction. From what I've gleaned elsewhere, At End of Day is likely inspired by the real-life chaos caused by Boston's Winter Hill Gang, who succeeded in playing the mob and law enforcement off each other for several decades while pursuing a barrage of virtually untraceable criminal enterprises across every level of society. We get a very good sense of that bewildering amount of activity here, but like the other later Higgins novels I've read, that level of ambition is frequently thwarted in reading comprehension by the author's stubborn insistence on telling the story entirely through dialogue. The speech may be beginning-to-end authentic, but when cops and robbers alike sound virtually the same, it's hard to tell who's chasing whom. This may be the point, though, and while teasing out a narrative among a succession of "scenes" may be challenging, I guess it does in fact entirely replicate the experience of being an FBI guy, say, listening to a RICO tape and trying to figure out who's saying what and, often to the fatality of many, why.
