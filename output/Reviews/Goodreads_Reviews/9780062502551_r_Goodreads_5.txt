'"Why must you poison the body in order to heal it?" he wanted to know. "If I were looking through the forest for plants that the Gods have given us for the treatment of cancer, I would look for something that would fortify the body rather than weaken it." Sastun is the real story of herbologist Rosita and her husband Greg moving to Belize and building a life for themselves there. The moment they start to question whether they should give up 'fighting' the jungle and go back home, Rosita finally meets Don Elijio Panti. She starts an apprenticeship with him, where he shares allhis knowledge about the flora (and its healing powers) of the Belizian jungle, and she learns much more about Mayan culture and the belief system, as Panti is not only a healer of physical qualms, but also mental/spiritual issues. This was such an unexpected delight. While it is a non-fiction book, the way Sastun is written very much feels like it is a fiction novel. I really enjoyed the blossoming friendship/mentorship between Rosita and Panti (who is an enigmatic and fascinating human to begin with). The anguish felt my Edijio about his way of life disappearing (both because there is no one learning how to do it, but also because farming and construction lay waste to large areas of jungle) was something that eminated from the pages and I could feel deeply. As a trained anthropologist, I also loved reading more about ancient Mayan culture and belief systems, family relations and the world of physical and spiritual healing. It gave me pause several times, making me question what I know (or believe to be true) about modern medicine. It's great to know that there are people (and projects) hard at work to protect the Panti's of the world.
