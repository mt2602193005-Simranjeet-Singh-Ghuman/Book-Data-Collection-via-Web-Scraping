"But now it is the present, timeless time, tense; only we pass, and that is some of the meaning, he supposes, of every story..." It's appropriate to (effectively?) conclude my project of reading every National Book Award winner with a little-known book that is fundamentally about the art of storytelling, weaving a novel and a children's story into the framework of a sort of meta-novel. How does one imbue a story with the nesting doll-like core of a even more distant past? How does it change for your audience and to suit your ego? When does one exert one's absolute power over it, and how? It's so natural to be caught up in the story of you and take for granted what lasts, "[t]he brave, eternal angle of her hip as she stands...melts his heart..." How easy it is to cause irreparable damage by being oneself ("Perhaps he was kind and didn't want to hurt her. But how do you not hurt women?"), and how inevitable it is that we will leave that wreckage behind, only to twist it into a tale of ourselves in the end.
