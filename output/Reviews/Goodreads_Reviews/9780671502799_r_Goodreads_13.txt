If the cover of my edition was a mashup it would be 'A Well Respected Man' and 'Lola', both by The Kinks. Both had been released in the five years before 'Sunday Best' was published (1971), with 'Lola' just the year before. It is a historical moment I find especially interesting, when the countercultural revolutions of the 1960s more widely surfaced in music, film and literature. Rubens had just won the second Booker Prize in 1970 on the descant theme of antipsychiatry (again very much of its moment), with 'Sunday Best' following in quick succession on transvestism (and at suggestive moments, transgenderism) in marriage. George Verrey Smith and his wife Joy alternate in the telling. This helps create balance, so that themes of gender identity, carried-through trauma and self-determination can play out in the binary of the relationship. The book avoids pity (it is in every sense, pitiless) just as it avoids othering. Rubens provides context for Smith's ritual Sunday feminisation sessions. The emergence of alter-ego 'Emily' follows, with a flight and new life that ensues as a double murder mystery one figurative, one real... The ability for George to 'pass' even at close range may be slightly far-fetched, even allowing for the strong make-up, hair and clothing outlines of the times. I've bumped it up from a 3.5, though, as it represents a relatively daring and sympathetic foray into themes that remain much discussed in the 2020s. Rubens writes unpleasant characters well, which unlike many authors, will stay with you. This could have been portrayed as kinky voyeurism. Instead, it's an empathetic take on real people living in hard times - much more like the (capitalised) Kinks.
