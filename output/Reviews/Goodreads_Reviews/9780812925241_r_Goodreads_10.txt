A fascinating historical artifact, but a problematic representation of autism While Somebody Somewhere is undeniably a landmark text in the history of autism literature, reading it today is a complicated experience. It is a sequel that tries to build a bridge to the neurotypical world, but often ends up reinforcing the idea of the autistic person as an incomprehensible "alien." Donna Williams is a gifted writer, but she often falls into the trap of what some critics call "autistic mysticism." The narrative is saturated with abstract metaphors, swirling colors, and intangible energies. While this may authentically reflect her internal state, as a reader, it often feels like wading through a fever dream or an acid trip. The prose can be self-indulgent, and the frequent insertion of song lyrics and poetry breaks the flow, obscuring the insight rather than clarifying it. You are asked to blindly accept an internal logic that is impossible to verify. The biggest caveat for the modern reader is the diagnostic validity. In the 90s, this was sold purely as an "autism memoir." However, Williams was later diagnosed with Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID). Looking back, much of the fragmentation, the distinct personas ('Willie' vs. 'Carol'), and the "checking out" of reality seem to point far more towards severe trauma and dissociation than towards autism itself. This makes the book a risky recommendation for those seeking to understand the general autistic experience. It conflates sensory processing issues with dissociative survival mechanisms, potentially confusing readers about what autism actually entails. An unrelatable "Super-Crip" narrative? Williams presents a very extreme, almost "magical" version of neurodivergence. Her ability to analyze her own subconscious with such rigorous, detached precision is rare, if not paradoxical. This creates a distance; it paints the autistic individual not as a person with a different operating system, but as a mystical puzzle to be solved. For many autistic readers, this highly specific, dramatized experience will feel alienating rather than validating. Read this as a unique psychological case study of a woman surviving a harrowing internal war, but do not read it as a textbook on autism. It is a product of its time—brave, but often impenetrable and diagnostically murky.
