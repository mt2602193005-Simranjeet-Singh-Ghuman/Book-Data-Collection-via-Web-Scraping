It was awe-inspiring to read of this intrepid individual’s solo journey by sailing boat around the world, beginning in 1895 for 3 years, 2 months, and 2 days (page 186 my edition). It was also a welcome reprieve from the daily blast of doomsday news (and books I read)! From his story, we gather he spent much of his life at sea, applying those experiences to his voyage and even built his sailing vessel almost from scratch (how many would be capable of doing that in this day and age?). He was 51 years old when he set out from Gloucester, near Boston. Given the storms and oceanic turmoil he underwent, the author strikes me as pretty much unflappable, more so when he battled the straits of Tierra del Fuego. Upon exiting on the Pacific side, he was forced back into the straits from the strong waves and winds. Most people would likely have given up at that stage, but not our sailor! He succeeded on the second exit on entering the Pacific Ocean. There are some rather crude and racist passages on Indigenous people he encountered. I found him to be a rather enigmatic character. We don’t learn a great deal about his background. The Wikipedia entry mentions he was married and had children at the time of his voyage, but we never know this from his book. Sadly, he sailed solo into the Atlantic Ocean in 1909 and was never seen again. The book is crowded with nautical terms, as if the author was more interested in navigation rather than observing the world and various people he encountered. There are some commentaries on those he met and the various landscapes he saw, but the nautical is predominant. It would be like travelling with a hiker who constantly describes the gear he wears and carries, instead of the scenery and fellow hikers. Nevertheless, this is a captivating read of an overwhelming journey.
