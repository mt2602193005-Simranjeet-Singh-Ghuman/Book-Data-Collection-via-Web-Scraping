This book outlines the collapse of the Roman Republic into the tyranny of one man. It begins with a brief overview of Rome's mythical founding in 753 BC; its rejection of monarchy in 509 BC; its imperial expansion up until 146 BC; and the adoption of murderous violence against popular elected figures. Rome's imperial expansion and embracement of political violence underpinned a state of perpetual civil war from 88 BC when Sulla first marched on Rome, until Octavian became Princeps of Rome in 31 BC. This period also witnessed Italian and slave revolts, Cicero defeating Cataline's conspiracy against the Republic, and Caesar's rise and fall. Although Holland simplifies this convoluted era of Roman history, the book feels rushed in some sections. Holland also proposes the thesis that Rome's descent into civil war and tyranny was primarily due to the cultural values ingrained in elite Roman men—glory at any cost. However, Holland does little to expound on this further, merely presenting it 'as is'. The book consequently does not appropriately analyse other potential causes put forth by other scholars—i.e., economic issues, the expansion of citizenship, or corruption. The reader may consequently be left with a skewed understanding of the reasons behind the collapse of the Roman Republic. Like his other work, "Persian Fire," Holland adopts a narrative historical style and frequently employs vernacular language. Perhaps due to this prose, the book has been commercially popular since its first publication two decades ago. Nevertheless, I find fault with this style for two reasons. First, it somewhat trivialises the extreme brutality of this period in Roman history, where millions died; and second, it lacks scholarly rigour by not thoroughly analysing the sources, which inherently contain biases and historical inaccuracies. As a result, this book is a reasonable starting point for novices to this period of history, but not as an academic source.
