Not a book about Andrew Jackson, nor really about the time period of Jackson's presidency (a book thus somewhat misnamed as "The Age of Jackson). Rather, this is a thesis-driven work that Jacksonian Democracy was a class movement against traditional elites and "the business community". This is counter to the traditional view of Jackson's rise as one of frontier populism. Eastern laborers are much more salient, on Schlesinger's account, than Western settlers. It's not entirely convincing: Jackson was extremely popular in the west, and "the business community", which is an ever-present bogey in the book, was surely just beginning to coalesce as commercial capitalism (much less industrial capitalism) was in its very early stages in the first half of the 19th century. Schlesinger is also somewhat confused on the issue of the bank war (as demonstrated by Bray Hammond in contemporary commentaries on Schlesinger), but perhaps that's apropos since the Jacksonians were likewise confused by banks (national, state, or otherwise) and their role not only in local commerce but national business cycles. This is understandable on the part of the Jacksonians, as economics and finance were not yet mature subjects of inquiry; Schlesinger's endorsement of the Jacksonian impulses in that area are less excusable.
