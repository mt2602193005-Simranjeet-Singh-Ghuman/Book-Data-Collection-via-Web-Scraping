Mark Ezra opens with a striking hook: Felicity Jardine, a retired former spy at the end of her emotional rope, has her planned suicide abruptly interrupted by a baby floating down a river. From that moment, instinct overtakes despair, and Felicity’s long-dormant training snaps back into place. It’s a powerful, almost cinematic opening, but what follows is far more intimate than action-driven. Felicity is a refreshing protagonist: older, world-weary, deeply capable, and haunted by her past. When an elderly neighbour who resembles her is found dead, it becomes clear that Felicity hasn’t faded into obscurity as thoroughly as she believed. The tension escalates as she’s forced to protect the baby, evade unseen enemies, and reconnect with two former MI6 colleagues, all while confronting the unresolved consequences of her Cold War-era work in 1970s Germany. Ezra excels at weaving timelines. The novel deftly links Felicity’s past operations to present-day danger, revealing how old missions never truly end, they just wait. The espionage elements are smart and grounded, but the emotional core is what lingers: guilt, identity, aging, and the cost of a life spent in secrecy. The pacing is deliberate but engaging, balancing investigative momentum with character-driven introspection. This isn’t a high-octane spy romp; it’s a thoughtful, tense unraveling of how history catches up with us, no matter how far we think we’ve run. Fans of John le Carré’s quieter moral complexity, alongside readers who enjoy mature protagonists and reflective thrillers, will find A Sting in Her Tale particularly rewarding. It’s a novel about second chances, earned, not gifted, and about finding purpose when you least expect to still need one.
