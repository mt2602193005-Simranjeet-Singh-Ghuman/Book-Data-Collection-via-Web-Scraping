“Beneath the Wheel” is a coming-of-age novel with many highs and lows. It has the dynamism of a river, which the one in the book is lacking. All the familiar banks pass by: the joys and sorrows of school, first friendships, first intoxication, first love. But Hesse recounts all this with such immediacy that one has the impression of drifting in the river oneself. Central to the story is the portrayal of a gifted young man who falls under the wheel in a society dominated by rules and conventions. He is less rebellious than his friend Heilner: at first, Hans Giebenrath appears to be a star who shines above all for his teachers. But they are not satisfied with that—he should shine even brighter! In their attempt to increase his luminosity, however, they end up dimming it. Ultimately, it is a story of extinction. And thus an indictment of those who sink stars in rivers and plunge the world into darkness. Hesse imbues his novel with a great deal of local color, which can sometimes be a little distracting. Nevertheless, it is an authentic narrative that fits seamlessly into the series of other great works about growing up: “The Sorrows of Young Werther,” “Spring Awakening,” “Godless Youth,” “The Confusions of Young Törless.” Ultimately, “Beneath the Wheel” also thrives on the ambivalence of adolescence: in retrospect, it oscillates between nostalgic idealization and relief that this period of world-weariness is over. At least it taught one how to swim. To some extent.
