2nd read. To be honest, before I started reading these classics, I was a pretty conservative Eastern soul who couldn’t stand all those “improper” romantic entanglements. But once I fell into them - whether it’s Anna Karenina or this 1 - The Red and the Black - those love-hate stories that challenge traditional moral boundaries kept stunning me with their charm. I found myself unknowingly surrendering to their narrative magic. Take this classic for example. Measured by moral standards, it’s basically the story of a classic “player” who climbs the social ladder by seducing women and ends up executed - a tale that should be satisfying and cathartic. But somehow, I found myself heartbroken with him at the end. It’s like the devil wrote this script! I’m sure over the past 2 centuries, there’s been a whole trainload of readers like me: we know Julien Sorel’s love life is “immoral,” but his boldness and twisted charm make it hard to condemn him. Julien’s background is tragic: son of a carpenter, pale and frail, useless at labor but brilliant at reading. He’s constantly beaten by his father and brothers. Luckily, his sharp mind earns him a recommendation to become a tutor in Mayor de Renal’s household - his “red” journey begins. There, 19-year-old Julien starts a romance with the nearly 30-year-old Madame de Renal. Later, to avoid scandal (and due to her guilty conscience), he’s sent to seminary, then recommended to the powerful Marquis de La Mole in Paris. Julien isn’t just good-looking - he’s smart, with a photographic memory and calm demeanor. He quickly becomes the Marquis’s favorite. During this time, he wins over the proud aristocrat Mathilde and even gets her pregnant. The Marquis, despite his displeasure, grants Julien a noble title and promotes him to cavalry lieutenant. Julien is on the brink of success. Then the drama hits - the Marquis receives a letter from Madame de Renal accusing Julien of seducing noblewomen for personal gain. Furious, the Marquis revokes everything. Julien, enraged, storms into a church and shoots Madame de Renal. He’s sentenced to death. Stendhal based the story on the 1827 Berthet murder case. Berthet, a poor young man, had affairs with 2 women and, after failing to secure a job, shot 1 of them in church. He shamelessly blamed her in court and was executed. Julien’s story mirrors Berthet’s, but literature reshapes reality. Berthet wanted to live and deflect blame; Julien seeks death and refuses to defend himself. Even more bizarre - Julien cries with joy upon learning Madame de Renal survived! This moment breaks free from the historical mold. Julien becomes a deeply conflicted, magnetic character - not just a killer, but a tortured idealist. Julien’s looks did help - Madame de Renal and her maid adored him, Mathilde fell for him, even a restaurant owner gave him free meals! Madame de Renal’s love got him into seminary; Mathilde’s got him into nobility. So yes, critics accused him of “climbing via women.” But look closer - his key promotions actually came from influential men - from carpenter’s son to tutor: thanks to Father Chelan, from tutor to seminary student: Chelan again, from seminary to noble secretary: Father Pirard’s recommendation. Why did these men favor him? Because Julien had real talent: photographic memory, Latin fluency, top grades, and relentless drive. His ambition matched the book’s title: red (military glory) and black (clerical power). Women and status were trophies for his vanity, not tools for manipulation. That’s Julien’s pride. But in my opinion, Julien’s feelings for the 2 women were both genuine. To Madame de Renal - a soulful love, a gentle refuge. At 19, Julien was shy and inexperienced. She was kind, class-blind, and motherly - a safe haven for a boy abused by his family. Their bond had shades of an Oedipal attachment. To Mathilde - a rational love, a battlefield. She was arrogant and dismissive. Julien, proud and sensitive, feared her scorn. But Mathilde was drawn to his restraint and pursued him. This proves Julien wasn’t scheming - if he were, Mathilde would’ve been his golden ticket. Instead, he was a clumsy, naive boy caught in her games, devastated by her hot-and-cold behavior. He even considered killing her and himself. His “seduction tactics” were learned from a Russian prince - hardly a seasoned player! His love was real: Madame de Renal soothed his soul; Mathilde sharpened his will. I read about W. Somerset Maugham criticized the ending as “illogical”- why would Julien shoot Madame de Renal if he’d diverged so far from Berthet? Well, to me, that’s Stendhal ’s genius. Julien receives the letter, buys a gun, enters the church, shoots, and surrenders - seeking death. The Marquis had offered him a fortune to leave France and forget Mathilde. Why did Julien choose ruin? Because Madame de Renal’s letter - written under clerical pressure - turned their love into a tool of poverty and greed. For Julien, who saw her as his first happiness, this betrayal shattered him. Madame de Renal, too, wanted to die - torn between religion and love, she couldn’t bear it. She hoped Julien would end it. After surviving, she fought to save him. In prison, Julien learns the truth, forgives her, and rediscovers pure intimacy. He realizes their time together was his peak of happiness. Ambition blinded him, but in death, he found love. He walks to the guillotine with peace. I believe Julien and Stendhal must be opposites. Julien was adored; Stendhal failed in love repeatedly. Julien longed for Napoleonic glory; Stendhal had served Napoleon but was indifferent to power. So Stendhal took his “sensitive, love-starved soul” from his “rough shell” and placed it in Julien’s beautiful body. Julien’s pride, sensitivity, and romantic triumphs were Stendhal ’s unreachable dreams. His final reconciliation with Madame de Renal echoed Stendhal ’s childhood longing for maternal love. Julien died fulfilled; Stendhal died alone on a Paris street. But he etched his deepest love into Julien’s tomb - and into his famous epitaph: Lived, wrote, loved. Isn’t that hauntingly beautiful? 5 / 5 stars
