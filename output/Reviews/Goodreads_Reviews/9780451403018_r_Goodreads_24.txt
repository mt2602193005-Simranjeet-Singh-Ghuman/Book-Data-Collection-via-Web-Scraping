Silk and Secrets completely blindsided me: A historical romance that opens in Istanbul and then travels across Central Asia to Kazakhstan was not something I ever expected to find - and yet here we are. I picked this up to tick Kazakhstan off my Read Around the World list and ended up utterly absorbed. Mary Jo Putney’s writing is rich, detailed, and immersive, especially when it comes to the Silk Road and the cultures encountered along it. This is an intense, sweeping romance that takes its setting seriously, and I was captivated by the sense of place as much as the story itself. At its heart, the plot follows Ross Carlisle, a seasoned Victorian explorer, who agrees to undertake a near-suicidal journey into Kazakhstan to discover the fate of his missing brother-in-law, accused of spying and presumed dead. Along the way, fate reunites him with his estranged wife Juliet, now living in the desert under an assumed identity, and the two are forced to travel together across hostile territory, joining a Silk Road caravan and navigating political danger, cultural tensions, and physical peril. Ross and Juliet are fascinating leads. When we meet them, they’ve been estranged for twelve years after marrying very young - Juliet at 17, Ross at 21. Ross has thrown himself into exploration, becoming the archetypal Victorian traveller, while Juliet has built a life for herself in the desert, earning the trust of local people and sometimes disguising herself as a man for survival and freedom. She is formidable, capable, and deeply compassionate. Their reunion is gradual, fraught, and wonderfully adult. This is a mature romance where the central conflict isn’t brushed aside or magically fixed. The pain, anger, and misunderstanding they’ve carried for over a decade are explored honestly, through conversation, reflection, and shared danger. I loved that they face the world as a team long before their marriage is fully mended. The adventure elements are vivid and memorable, from grumpy camels and caravan travel to cultural encounters such as religious differences, Buzkashi (a sport using a goat carcass) and real peril, but the emotional core never gets lost. Ross’ honour and steadiness balance beautifully against Juliet’s fierce independence and passion. I listened to the audiobook, narrated by Siobhan Waring, and she was excellent. Her clear diction and measured delivery felt perfectly suited to both the era and the sweeping scope of the story, and she handled the nuance with real care. This was my first Mary Jo Putney novel, and I didn’t feel disadvantaged by starting here. I enjoyed it so much that I fully intend to continue with the series and explore more of her backlist. An unexpectedly brilliant find.
