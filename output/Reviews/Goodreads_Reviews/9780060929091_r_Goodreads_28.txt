“Bushidō means ‘the way of the warrior’, a Japanese samurai concept. The idea is, the samurai achieves total detachment by seeing himself as already dead.” Bushidō becomes a guiding principle for many of the novel’s characters in this sort of Zen Noir, illustrating the novel’s deep concern with identity, violence, and the possibility of transcendence through detachment. Already Dead is structurally ambitious and philosophically saturated, in the best possible way. The timeline shifts without warning, voices overlap, and at one point, a pivotal revelation arrives in a long handwritten letter that delivers deranged exposition. The plot was confusing at times, but I eventually locked into its mosaic quality. The prose is exactly what I have come to expect from DJ…blazing, feverish, often messianic. Johnson writes not just to describe but to elevate, to pull the visible world into the realm of the symbolic. His language veers from the lush to the incantatory. He isn’t afraid to slow the story down for a few pages to ask: What is a soul? Why do we destroy what we love? Beneath the beauty and madness runs that cold Bushidō logic: the clearest thought, the boldest act, comes only when the fear of death is extinguished. To be “already dead,” in Johnson’s world, is to be finally free, however brutal or broken that freedom may look.
