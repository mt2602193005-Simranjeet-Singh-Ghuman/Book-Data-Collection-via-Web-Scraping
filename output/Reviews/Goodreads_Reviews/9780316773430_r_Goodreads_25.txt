They don't make historians like Arthur Schlesinger any more, for better and for worse. This glorious yet flawed account of the age, not just the presidency, of Andrew Jackson, can be read on two levels; in fact, it must be. Young Schlesinger, bedazzled by Franklin Delano Roosevelt, saw an intriguing parallel between the two presidents he regarded, and highly, as champions of the people against the money interests. Where Jackson waged war on the Second Bank of the United States, Roosevelt took on Wall Street. (A curious irony; Nicholas Biddle, president of the Second Bank and bitter foe of Jackson was the ancestor of Frances Biddle, FDR's Attorney General.) Jackson fought with Supreme Court Chief Justice John Marshall for blocking his economic program and Roosevelt attempted to pack the Supreme Court over the same cause. Jackson made the Whig Party irrelevant and Roosevelt almost, not quite, vanquished the Republicans. Both presidents created a new consensus in American politics that outlasted their presidencies and their lifetimes; wealth redistribution, widening of the suffrage and expansion of the reach of the federal government, with more power vested in the presidency. This is first rate liberal Democratic history without apology. Schlesinger votes for Jackson, and FDR, on every page. Shame on him for not once mentioning Jackson's forced removal of the American Indian or holding of slaves and defending slavery.
