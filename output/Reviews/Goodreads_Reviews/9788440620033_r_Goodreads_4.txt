The Gravity of Disappointment: A Review of Dan Simmons' Weakest Link Dan Simmons is the celebrated author behind the magnificent Hyperion Cantos and the Ilium/Olympus cycles. Early in his career, specifically in 1985, he published the horror novel Song of Kali, which earned him the World Fantasy Award. It is a haunting exploration of the Kāpālika cult and a meditation on a humanity dominated by hatred and violence—themes perfectly embodied, in the narrator’s mind, by the squalor and chaos of Calcutta. It is a "terrible" book in every sense of the word: both horrific in content and devastating in its execution. Simmons's versatility is well-documented across a prolific bibliography that includes Carrion Comfort, The Terror, The Fifth Heart, and numerous other works. Having read nearly his entire body of work, I can say without hesitation that Phases of Gravity is his weakest effort. Published in 1989, just a few years after the Challenger disaster, Phases of Gravity is a work of speculative fiction centred on an ex-astronaut navigating a midlife crisis. Unfortunately, the narrative is bogged down by exhaustive aeronautical technical descriptions. These passages are filled with dense jargon and explanations that, at least for me, lacked any compelling interest. Ironically, the technical accuracy is perhaps the only redeeming quality of the book. The storytelling itself is problematic. There is no discernible plot to anchor the reader, and the timeline is needlessly jarring. Simmons frequently flashes back to the past and returns to the present within a single paragraph, causing the narrative to bounce back and forth rather than flow. I often found myself confused, struggling to discern the context of a given scene. While I am no expert on the life of an astronaut, if Simmons’ depiction bears even a passing resemblance to reality, it suggests a subject so uninteresting that it fails to justify a novel. What is most baffling is the dip in quality from an author who has proven, time and again, to be a master storyteller. Ultimately, at least I didn't lose much time. I finished the book in three days and promptly returned it to the dust of my "forgotten" shelf.
