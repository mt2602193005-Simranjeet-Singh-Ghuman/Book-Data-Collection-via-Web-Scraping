A Western with a difference. Miss Morissa, with many emotional hangups behind her, comes West to Nebraska in the 1870s to live with her stepfather after completing her medical degree. But the stepfather, a rancher in the mountains, where rumours of gold have already brought in the ragtag dregs, the cattle rustlers and horse thieves, has already decided to move from these now dangerous badlands. Miss Morissa, however, taking the view that cannot depend on her stepfather all her life, decides to stay on as the “lady doc.” Morissa’s difficulties are sometimes unexpected. When called to attend a pregnant woman, Morissa's horror is unfeigned at the thought of a woman in such a situation in this wild country, with unfriendly Indians added to the inhospitable territories of the outlaws. Despite her struggle against typhoid, mountain fever and even smallpox, against accidents and gunshot sounds and barroom brawls, the doctor is not universally accepted. Her steady insistence in turning her small holding into a hospital attracts the hostility of the settlement, who tend to see it as a source of infection, not healing. Secondly Morissa, in a fit of pique, has married an outright criminal, and the Sheriff accuses her of harbouring outlaws and murderers. The novel has extraordinary persons peopling it: Wild Bill Cody, Calamity Jane, who makes Morissa’s life a misery for months afterwards with a few taunts, offset by her fellow doctors who can't praise her enough for the outstanding work she has accomplished in some of wildest, the most lawless lands of the West. Among them is the young Dr Walter Reed himself. Feminism, courage, and simple humanism mark this novel as outstanding.
