In British maritime history of the late eighteenth century, few events captured the public's imagination more than the Mutiny on the Bounty. There have been at least two films made & these positioned Fletcher Christian as a wronged man standing up for his crewmates against a cruel Captain (actually Lieutenant on this voyage). This book, based on records & testimonies from the court martials etc, argues that far from being cruel, William Bligh tried to avoid discipline by the usual method of flogging as much as possible which throws doubt on Christian's motives for the mutiny. Plus almost half the crew wanted to leave with the Lieutenant, something unlikely if he was as capricious as reported afterwards, mostly by people who had a grudge against Bligh it must be said. The author opens with the remaining mutineers (those who stayed in Tahiti rather than sailing to Pitcairn with Christian) being rounded up & taken back to England for trial. We then go back to start at the beginning of the story, through the mutiny, the subsequent trials, & finding out what happened to the major players. Despite the best efforts of the author, the narrative unfortunately became a little dry in places. My takeaway from this book is that although Bligh had his faults, I tend to think that perhaps his reputation has been unfairly traduced over the years. Overall - good account of the story but can become overly bogged down in the details & academically dry in places. 2.5 stars (rounded up)
