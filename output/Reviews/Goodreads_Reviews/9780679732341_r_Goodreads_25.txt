I found this book underwhelming and just okay. The parts I did find to be of interest also happened to be very depressing, as pretty much every character with the exception of Laura was pitiful and sad. I guess that's a function of the fact that the narrator is one of those classic "disaffected youth" types, where he can't help but see the pathos in everyone he encounters. I could see why reviewers compared it to "Catcher in the Rye," because Charles had that attitude that he was somehow better than others, more observant and more capable of seeing through bullshit, but it was an attitude that disguised an unspoken self-loathing. Yeah, Charles was pretty much a grown-up Holden Caulfield, minus the money and the New York setting. The ending didn't really work for me. I mean, it fit with Charles' character, which was to be completely oblivious and self-involved, but it seemed to come out of left field with Laura, who up til that moment had showed no inclination that she would do what she did. Was Beattie aiming for a happy ending? I didn't feel like it was happy. I thought it was probably the most depressing part of the book, which is saying something, as all of the passages involving Charles' mother and stepfather had me cringing. There were moments in which I really enjoyed this book, where the observations were uncomfortably sharp, but a lot of the time I just didn't care. I kept reading because I felt a sense of obligation to do so, because it's Ann Beattie OMG, but man, I found it completely tedious. I'll read more of her stuff, because I like her New Yorker stories, but this book just did not do it for me. I think the fact that it took me nearly three weeks to read 250 pages tells you everything you need to know.
