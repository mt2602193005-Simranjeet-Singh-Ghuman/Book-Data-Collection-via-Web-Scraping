With any collection, readers will always complain, "the editor should have included this poem, or shouldn't have included that one." However, the choices that editor, Richard Sylvester, has made here are spot on for the purposes of introducing readers key verses of the period and to facilitate the reader's enjoyment in identifying common tropes and references across works. I have read most of these verses many times over, but had never read the collection entirely, in order, and this provided a unique and enjoyable experience. Poets looking for inspiration will find great diversity. Readers can discover the didactic wisdom of Gascoigne, whose "Certayne notes of Instruction concerning the making of verse or rhyme in English," provides great insight into Renaissance poetic plumbing. Conversely, readers can ponder Sydney's contrary (and probably somewhat disingenuous) guidance to not "turn others' leaves," but instead "looke in they heart and write." Sylvester keeps the poets' original, archaic phrasing and spelling, which takes some discipline on the part of the reader. In the introduction, he states that this slows down readers, so they think more about what is happening in every poem, while also ensuring that potential meaning is not lost, as happens whenever a translator makes a choice to change anything. Sylvester's collection is definitely intended for students and scholars. The material is challenging enough that I would personally recommend casual readers to pick up modern translations.
