** 1/2 Capitalizing on the early 1970s fascination with ESP and mind-reading, genre stalwart Robert Silverberg turns out a novel with little speculative fodder and much inner-city angst. The concept of the mind-reading loser may have found riper territories under the pen of comic misanthropes Stanley Elkin, Roth, or even Edward Lewis Wallant (The Tenants of Moonbloom), but here Silverberg can't help escape the juvenile narrative of a gifted loner, David Selig, who makes a living writing papers for Columbia University's athletes and rich kids. David can't find romance even though he has the power to eavesdrop on his target's inner desires; flat-lines his professional life down to near zero; and has a burdensome kinship with a hip sister who despises him and seems to be in relationships with half the city's intellectuals. Sounds quite promising on the spectrum of loser-fiction, but I felt mired in the shallow waters of Selig's first-person winching and whining, leaving the readers entangled in a diary entry of someone who fucked and fudged their miraculous skill on repeat. Sure, he's meant to be un-likeable, the inevitable asshole born in Brooklyn, but really, there is little ground to stand on. 'Dying Inside' felt like a missed opportunity, a bag of gas that could have been dynamite. What is left is only the parable of having a gift and letting it tarnish, wither and then simply wash away to black. But meaningful parable aside, this is only a fair novel, a gem of an idea that never quite transcends itself into either heartbreak or epiphany (still, the LSD scene is quite something). Oh yeah, and there's the usual Silverberg boobisms at play (the man has a disruptive breast fetish) as well as some silly banter aka jive talk when it comes to race relations. But at 200 pages, it's a digestible and disappointing kibitz.
