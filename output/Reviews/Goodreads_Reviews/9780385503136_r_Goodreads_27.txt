I enjoyed this book but I am not in any position to comment on Tom Holland as a competent historian of the period (I hated his history of Christianity to such an extent that it stopped me from reading this book for at least two years) but it is readable and for those new to Roman history it is probably not a bad place to start. Clearly any book whose opening paragraph has lines like these: "Rather than gesture his men onward, Gaius Julius Caesar instead gazed into the turbid waters of the Rubicon, and said nothing. And his mind moved upon silence. "The Romans had a word for such a moment Discrimen, they called it--an instant of perilous and excruciating tension, when the achievements of an entire lifetime might hang in the balance. The career of Caesar, like that of any Roman who aspired to greatness, had been a succession of such crisis points. Time and again he had hazarded his future--and time and again he had emerged triumphant. This, to the Romans, was the very mark of a man.” is bound to raise the hackles of anyone who likes to separate history from fiction writing but this egregious slip does not reflect the rest of the book - though obviously this is popular not academic history but then I don't see anything wrong with popular history when it largely sticks to what is known and is not trying force modern relevance/parallels indiscriminately on material it doesn't fit (see for example almost all the works of Neil Fergusson for how not to write history). Holland does indulge some slight attempts to find modern relevance in the story of the fall of the Roman Republic and the rise of the Caesar's and Imperial Rome but fortunately he doesn't overplay it and confines such speculations to the introduction. For me this was good popular history, I enjoyed it immensely, but wouldn't dream of taking it as a definitive account.
