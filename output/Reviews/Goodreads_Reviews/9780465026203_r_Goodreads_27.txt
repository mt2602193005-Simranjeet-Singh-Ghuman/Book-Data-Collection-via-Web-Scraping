The second, Walt Riff rifling through the scattered secondhand volumes in a charmless cheap motel room, naturally sparked my enthusiasm, as I'll survey any bookshelf jumbled in any accommodation. Its abundance of geegaws and gimcrack Midwestern soaps, tracts, and B+B ephemera carries inherent interest, but there's again not the momentum to propel any revelation amidst a soporific atmosphere. So, I headed the downhill stretch not expecting any dynamic shift in the sparsely told but snappier Peder "Emma Enters a Sentence of Elizabeth Bishop's" which evoked, not for the first time in Gass, the heartland dread and spare delineation of Sherwood Anderson. Its climax isn't as shocking as Gass might have reckoned for us jaded readers after decades of postmodernism, but it's an efficient tale. Luther Pinner by his moniker in the concluding novella begins his career of revenge on his schoolboy tormenters promisingly. Echoes a grotesque out of William Gaddis, Alexander Theroux, or Thomas Pynchon in their erudite, maximalist, unrestrained comeuppance of clods, bullies, frauds, and jerks. But the storyline for all its comparative exuberance doesn't warm the cockles, but plods desultorily.
