The edition of the book that I read ends with an epilogue dated 1996, a full two years before the fateful and still-holding Good Friday Agreement that brought an effective end to the Northern Ireland Troubles, notwithstanding the dreadful Omagh bombing of later that same year (1998). Coogan is decidedly sympathetic to the Nationalist (aka Catholic) point of view, not so much because of sympathy for its goal of a fully united, 32-county Irish republic, as because of his recognition that Catholics living in the six counties under British rule have been treated shabbily by both their Unionist (aka Protestant) neighbors and the British government. The resurgence of the Irish Republican Army as a fighting force in the late 60s/early 70s, and its subsequent development into one of the most feared and effective guerilla militias ever, increased both the stakes and the urgency of finding political solutions to the divisions that continued to wound both the six counties and the neighboring Republic of Ireland. The book ends with the prescient observation that demographics held forth the brightest reason for optimism for a just outcome as Catholics were rapidly moving from a minority to a majority in the region, owing largely to immigration from Eastern Europe and a penchant for young Protestants to tend to their fortunes on the larger island to the east. I think even Coogan must have been somewhat surprised to see Sinn Fein eventually seated as a majority party at Stormont. The prospects for the ultimate Nationalist goal of a single flag flying over all of Ireland have perhaps never been better, and the unpopularity of Brexit among the six counties didn't hurt, but there is still a long way to go before that goal is reached, if it ever is, but this book is a helpful primer for those who need reminding of how far the region has come, and how much it has to lose should it ever backtrack.
