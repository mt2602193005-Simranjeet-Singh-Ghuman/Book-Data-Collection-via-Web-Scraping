“But you cannot go on ‘explaining away’ forever: you will find that you have explained explanation itself away.” C.S. Lewis’s THE ABOLITION OF MAN is not an easy book to digest. I found this short work dense, layered, and demanding. It is the kind of text that requires slow, careful reading. I’m glad I approached it that way. In this book, Lewis argues for the necessity of universal moral values as the foundation for both individual character and human society. Without shared, objective standards, which he calls the Tao, civilization cannot endure. Lewis is not merely offering philosophical reflections; he is sounding an alarm. The first chapter, “Men Without Chests,” engages literature and education, critiquing the modern tendency to reduce values to subjective feelings. For readers unfamiliar with literary theory or the intrinsic value of art as a discipline, this chapter may feel particularly challenging. Lewis insists that education must train not only the intellect (the head) and the appetites (the belly), but also the rightly ordered affections (the chest). When that moral center is removed, we do not create enlightened people, we create hollow ones. The final chapter, “The Abolition of Man,” is the key to the entire work. Here, Lewis warns that humanity’s attempt to conquer nature through science untethered from morality or through what he provocatively likens to magic, will ultimately result in the conquest of humanity itself. When man seeks total control over reality without submitting to objective moral law, he does not become more human; he becomes less. The crux of Lewis’s argument is captured in the book’s closing line: “It is no use trying to ‘see through’ first principles. If you see through everything, then everything is transparent. But a wholly transparent world is an invisible world. To ‘see through’ all things is the same as not to see.” By “first principles,” Lewis means those foundational moral truths that ground human interaction and dignity. If we insist on explaining them away (reducing them to evolutionary impulses, social constructs, or psychological conditioning) we undermine the very framework that allows meaning to exist. A world stripped of mystery, reverence, and moral absolutes becomes a world without depth. And a world without depth ultimately becomes unlivable. From a theological perspective, this resonates deeply. Scripture often speaks of “mystery” in reference to the character of God and the wisdom embedded in creation. Lewis’s warning is clear- when humanity tries to conquer or dissolve that mystery, when we attempt to bend ultimate realities to our own will, we set ourselves on a destructive path. History, and contemporary culture, seems to affirm Lewis’s concerns more each day. The appendix of the book strengthens the text’s central argument beautifully. Lewis compiles moral teachings from diverse cultures (Asian, Roman, Norse, and many others) demonstrating that throughout history, across civilizations, there has been a consistent moral framework. Though expressed differently, these cultures share a recognition of universal laws governing justice, courage, honesty, and self-sacrifice. As a Christian, I found this appendix particularly compelling. It shows that moral law is not an invention but something written into the human heart. These shared principles transcend religion, culture, and time. They point to a Creator who has embedded moral awareness into humanity itself. Quotes: • “No emotion is, in itself, a judgment; in that sense all emotions and sentiments are alogical. But they can be reasonable or unreasonable as they conform to Reason or fail to conform. The heart never takes the place of the head: but it can, and should, obey it. • “In a sort of ghastly simplicity we remove the organ and demand the function. We make men without chests and expect of them virtue and enterprise. We laugh at honor and are shocked to find traitors in our midst. We castrate and bid the geldings be fruitful.” • “The human mind has no more power of inventing a new value than of imagining a new primary color, or, indeed, of creating a new sun and a new sky for it to move in.” • “An open mind, in questions that are not ultimate, is useful. But an open mind about the ultimate foundations either of Theoretical or of Practical Reason is idiocy.” • “My point is that those who stand outside all judgments of value cannot have any ground for preferring one of their own impulses to another except the emotional strength of that impulse.” • “But once our souls, that is ourselves, have been given up, the power thus conferred will not belong to us.” Lewis was a profound thinker and a careful writer. What saddens me is the suspicion that many today would read THE ABOLITION OF MAN and struggle to understand it. Not because it lacks clarity, but because it demands moral seriousness and philosophical depth. It calls readers to think beyond themselves and to grapple with foundational truths. That alone makes it a book worth reading.
