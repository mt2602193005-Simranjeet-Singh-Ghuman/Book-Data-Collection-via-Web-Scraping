Meriwether Lewis (1774 – 1809) is the central figure in this detailed account of the Lewis & Clark expedition (1803-1806). It starts with minibiographies of both Lewis and William Clark (1770 – 1838). It discusses Lewis’s work as an aide to President Thomas Jefferson, and how the journey was initially envisioned. It was deemed particularly important at the time to find a water route to the Pacific Ocean. The main body of the group consisted of military soldiers, and Lewis and Clark acted as co-Captains. They departed from St. Louis, traveled up the Missouri River, built a fort and made a winter camp with the Mandan tribe, crossed the Rocky Mountains, and reached the Pacific near modern-day Astoria. The company also included young Shoshone woman Sacagawea (c.1788 – 1812), her infant child Jean-Baptiste, her French-Canadian trader husband Toussaint Charbonneau, a Black servant called York, and pathfinder George Drouillard. The narrative covers the expedition's encounters with many Native American tribes, near-starvation in the mountains, scientific observations of flora and fauna, and mapmaking across previously uncharted territory. Ambrose has used expedition journals, particularly Lewis's entries, to reconstruct events. The author portrays Lewis as brilliant but troubled. He was able to thrive during the journey but had significant issues once he returned. Ambrose highlights how the expedition's role in American westward expansion and scientific inquiry. was not a trained scientist, but his descriptions of plants and wildlife stand up to the standards of the day. He described many previously unknown species. I enjoyed the author’s storytelling and use of primary sources. It was the first time I had read a detailed account of the entire expedition, and it was quite an accomplishment. I found it interesting that Lewis and Jefferson were keen on inviting tribal leaders to meet with Jefferson in Washington DC. They definitely underutilized Sacagawea, especially regarding first contact with tribes where the expedition leaders did not speak the language. If you are interested in the goals and accomplishments of the Lewis & Clark expedition, and the details of their journey, I don’t think you can go wrong here. 4.5 ************************************************************** Interesting tidbits: – The translation process was often complicated. For example, Drouillard could translate the captains’ English to French, understood by Charbonneau, who could speak in Hidatsa to his wife Sacagawea, who could talk with her people in Shoshone. Sign language was used extensively. One can only imagine the “telephone” chain of misunderstandings that surely must have arisen. – Sacagawea spoke both Shoshone and Hidatsa. She served as a link in the communication chain during some crucial negotiations but received nothing for her troubles at the journey’s end (all the men were paid, of course). She carried her infant on a traveling board on her back throughout the journey. She was the only woman in the company and was crucial in making the tribes feel comfortable since they viewed a group traveling with a woman (and baby) as having friendly intentions.
