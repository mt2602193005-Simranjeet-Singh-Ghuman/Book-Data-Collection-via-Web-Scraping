Minor spoilers ahead. It left me wanting to bang my head against the wall — and I am not even exaggerating. What should have been a somewhat neutral review turned into a fierce need to speak my heart out about this agonising, overly dramatic circus of pages and pages of thoughts and so few lines of actual dialogue that I actually forgot these people were supposed to live in a place and a time. For every five lines of dialogue, we get — without exaggeration — about three pages of craving, lusting, longing, inner battles, and, eventually, suicidal thoughts. This “transformation” happened after my least unlikeable character, Stuart, had a fight with his father (who also happens to be the one who speaks the most in actual dialogue). If this pseudo-philosophical novel had possessed a sustainable, enduring plot, I might have cried. Instead, I just felt my heart pounding and aching in disgust. Do people really act like this? Can someone say such horrible things for reasons like these? I’ll stop here for spoiler reasons, but the scene had a tragic impact on my poor heart, which never expected anything in this story to shake it that much. My boy Stuart, who seemed both witty and foolish at the same time, didn’t appear to care very much. Midge, my least favourite character, turns out to be the catalyst, and I desperately wanted her to run away somewhere and disappear. Of course, she doesn’t. She actually returns stronger than ever, armed with what feels like hundreds of pages of remorse that, ironically, only turn her back into her old self — and that made me extremely angry. Angry at her for uttering rubbish in front of a character who only existed as a target. Stuart is the most talked-about character, the most spitefully treated, and one of the few reasons for genuine dialogue and spoken — not cherished and confined — emotional states. Stuart’s fortune is that he truly doesn’t give a shit — even less than I do, the silent witness of his sad, misread life. Reveries and mysticism — too many reveries and too many mystical moments in this melodramatic mess. These characters are made of thoughts; they are thoughts. I don’t know what their passions are, what they do in their free time. I know Thomas mows the lawn, but I’m not entirely convinced the rest of them even exist. Everything feels like Edward is having a fever dream and could wake up at any moment from his own overthinking. No, I did not take the tragedy lightly — the tragedy and that other thing (are these spoilers if they happen in the first hundred pages and are then endlessly repeated until the end?). They genuinely affected me. But at some point, a flock of words invaded my brain, exhausted me, and drained both my excitement and my will to finish the book. I had clearly imagined a very different picture of Iris Murdoch’s style than what this novel delivered. I knew inner thoughts played a major role in her writing, but I didn’t expect them to become so overwhelming. I deeply regret my misunderstanding of what this book was supposed to be. It left me with a few headaches. I don’t know if I’m in a position to say whether I would recommend it or not, since I’m still unsure if I’m the right type of reader for Murdoch. Still searching for her style, still wondering whether this simply wasn’t an interesting enough theme for me — though I might still enjoy some of her other books. I’m planning to read A Severed Head, with less enthusiasm, but the book is waiting, so I guess I’ll give it a try. P.S. I wrote this review while still having a hundred pages left to read, and I have nothing else to add. Everything turns into a continuous, wide-open happy ending — just what you need after so many harsh words, induced psychosis, signs, and spirituality. The happy ending doesn’t change anything about the heartache I felt. Maybe Stuart managed to get over it, but I can’t — for the sake of the single character who made me believe in this muck.
