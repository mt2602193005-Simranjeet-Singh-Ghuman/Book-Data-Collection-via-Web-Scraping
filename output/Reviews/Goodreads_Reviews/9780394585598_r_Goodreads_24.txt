Very well researched, and largely written in an engaging and dramatic way, Sam Tanenhaus’s biography of Whittaker Chambers is a fascinating book: the two trials and the long House Committee on Un-American Activities sessions and FBI investigations read like a spy thriller, with Chambers occasionally fibbing and occasionally telling dark truths about himself and his friends. These sections - the longest in the book - are hard to out down, even some 70 years after they happened and all the principles are long dead. The rest of the book is well researched and written, although lacks the same spunk and drive. Perhaps Chambers wasn’t especially interesting in his later years. Still, anyone interested in this period of American history will find a lot here to chew on; maybe pair it with Murray Kempton’s book Part of Our Time: Some Ruins and Monuments of the Thirties for a more complete look.
