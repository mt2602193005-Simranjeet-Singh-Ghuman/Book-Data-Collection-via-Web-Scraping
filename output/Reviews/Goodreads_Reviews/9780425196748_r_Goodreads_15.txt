This is the first Dick Francis book I have read in years and I realize how much I miss him. For twenty years I could annually read a reliably excellent, engrossing mystery in which an often bruised hero lived a fascinating, outwardly quiet life adjacent to the British horse racing world. In Wild Horses we meet Tony Lyon, a film director who has no special set of skills that see him beating up younger thugs at will. Instead he is a classic Francis character, a man of decency, dependable and brave in ways most of those around him will never know the details of. Another aspect of Dick Francis that comes through is his lifelong ability to educate himself. His bio shows little schooling. He clearly used his intellect to rise to the top of two highly competitive fields: racing and writing. Having presumably been involved in film adaptations of his books, he acquired a deep understanding of whole professions. I learned a lot about the job of being a screen actor, the rehearsal, the minute facial movements registering an emotion, the gravitas exuded on film. The cinematographer and director collaborating in planning a shot and then the real time adjustments to account for a flubbed line or to take advantage of a passing storm cloud. Dick Francis, a man who worked hard to achieve tremendous success in multiple fields, remained humble, both ostensibly in his personal life, and in the characters he chose to animate. In this era, 2026, of bombastic self aggrandizing blowhards to whom television cameras insatiably turn, reading an old Dick Francis is a balm and a reminder of what a great man is.
