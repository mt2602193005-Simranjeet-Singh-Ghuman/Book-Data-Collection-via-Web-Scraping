Black Beauty was a story I fell in love with, around the same time I had my first pony lesson, back in 1989! Reading it now as an adult feels like I am recalling something ancient. Something wordless that only horse-loving people would truly understand. Anna Sewell somehow reached into that quiet, sacred space between horse and human and gave it a voice. This is a deeply moving story told through the eyes of a horse as he journeys through kindness, cruelty, love, and loss at the hands of different owners. More than just a children's classic, it is a quiet meditation on compassion, dignity, and the sacred bond between humans and animals. Through Beauty's life, Sewell reveals not only the emotional depth of horses, but the true nature of the people who handle them. The story holds many sweet lessons on kindness, courage, and hope - for readers both young and old. Having spent most of my life in racing yards, around young horses being backed and schooled, I've always believed horses can see straight through us. They don't care who you pretend to be. They respond to energy, to softness, to fear, to patience. They carry our chaos without ever asking to. Reading this book again brought all of that back to the surface for me in a way I wasn't expecting. It made me think about every horse that ever trusted me enough to lower its head to my chest, every frightened youngster trying to understand the human world, every pair of eyes that seemed to hold something almost unbearably wise. There's a deep grief running through this book, but also something profoundly spiritual. Beauty moves through human hands the way so many horses do in life: loved by some, misunderstood by others, used, cherished, discarded, rescued. And through it all, he remains gentle and trying. That broke my heart a little. Horses have this incredible ability to absorb human cruelty without losing their capacity for trust entirely. I think that's why they change people. To truly know a horse is, to me, to be confronted by your own soul. What resonated most is that the story isn't really about horses at all, it's about what kind of people we choose to be. The horses in the book mirror humanity back to itself. The kind hands heal, the cruel hands destroy, and the horse remembers all of it. I always felt that sense of wisdom whenever I was in their presence. Many times in my life, I shared with them my worries, my fears, and my hopes - all without me ever having to say a word. Reading Black Beauty alongside my own memories felt almost painful at times, because horses have always represented something bigger in my life than sport or work. They were freedom, survival, companionship, grounding... In many ways they carried parts of me I couldn't carry myself. There's a line between human and horse that sometimes disappears entirely, especially when you've spent years living by their rhythms: the cold mornings, the silence of the stable before dawn, the feeling of breathing in sync with another creature. It became less of a job to me and more of a way of existing in the world. That's what the story captured for me so beautifully, the quiet sacredness of horses. Their dignity. Their forgiveness. Their presence. Anna Sewell understood that horses are not here merely to serve us. They walk beside us for a brief time, and if we're lucky (as I was), they leave us softer, wiser, and more human that we were before. Some books are read, while others are felt somewhere much deeper. For me, Black Beauty belongs to the second kind - the kind I hold dearest to me. 🐴
