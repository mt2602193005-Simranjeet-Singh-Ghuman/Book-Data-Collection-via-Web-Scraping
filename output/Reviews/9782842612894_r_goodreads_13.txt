Modou is dead. How am I to tell you? One does not fix appointments with fate. Fate frasps whom it wants, when it wants. When it moves in the direction of your desires, it brings you plenitude. But more often than not, it unsettles, crosses you. Then one has to endure. Ramatoulaye is writing a long letter, almost a diary, to her close friend Aissantou. She is in seclusion to mourn the death of her estranged husband, Modou, for forty days. However, she has been mourning the loss of her marriage for five years when Modou married a younger second wife. He left their home, abandoning her and their twelve children. Ramatoulaye is a teacher living in Senegal, a Muslim country in western Africa, where polygamous marriages are allowed. Her best friend, Aissantou, was in the same situation, but she divorced her husband instead of living in a polygamous home. Aissantou found a new occupation and a new life in America. Many Senegalese traditions devalue the status of women. When a man dies, the possessions in the home are taken by the in-laws at a financially difficult time. Traditionally, a widow might be taken as an additional wife by her husband's brother. But Ramatoulaye refused the brother because she did not love and respect him. She was an intelligent and independent woman. The importance of education for women was also emphasized. Both Ramatoulaye and Aissantou were educated, worked as teachers, and made sure that their children attended good schools. Education gave them options that other abandoned women did not possess. "So Long a Letter" was first published in 1979. Author Mariama Ba saw Senegal transition from being a French colony to gaining independence in 1960. Senegal was trying to enter the modern world while still retaining Muslim traditions. Mariama Ba was a supporter of women's rights, and this excellent epistolary novel is semi-autobiographical.
